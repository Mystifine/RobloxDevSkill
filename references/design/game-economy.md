# Game economy and live ops

Companion to [game-design.md](game-design.md): load it when a design or a system touches currencies, pacing, odds or the update cadence. Robux products, prices, offers and the paid random items policy are in [monetization.md](monetization.md). Its results go in the plan's Economy and Live calendar sections ([assets/templates/GAME_DESIGN_PLAN.md](../../assets/templates/GAME_DESIGN_PLAN.md)).

## Economy and pacing

- **Few currencies:** one soft (earned by the core loop, spent on power), one premium or rare (earned slowly, may be bought), and a material only when it enables a distinct decision (shards that evolve units). At most three on the HUD; each has sources, sinks, a place on screen and a reason.
- **Faucets and sinks**, tabled per phase with rates per minute. Sinks scale with income, or currency piles up; rich players get fantasy sinks (cosmetics, rerolls, fusions).
- **Price an unlimited reroll at about what the set it rerolls pays:** simulated with the shop's own prices as the exchange rate, a set of five random rewards was worth 52.5% of the next unlock, while the first reroll price was 15%: an unlimited reroll at under a third of its value. The price went to 50%.
- **Every consumable a game grants needs a spend path in the client:** egg tickets were granted by daily and playtime rewards and accepted by the server's egg-open handler (a `payWith` field), yet every client call sent `payWith = "wins"`, so tickets piled up unspendable; grepping the request's payload field across the client code found it. When adding or auditing a consumable, trace its grant, the client's spend path and the server's: a grant with no client spend path is a dead currency.
- **Offline earnings:** a share of the online rate (10-50%) up to a cap (2-12 h), paid with a welcome-back moment; online stays clearly better. One game paid a quarter of the time away as idle training, capped at 6 h earned over 24 h away; selling a doubling of it: [offers.md](offers.md).
- **Collectibles multiply the main stat** *(default)*: in a simulator, every collectible tier (auras, pets) is usually a multiplier source, and players expect it: one owner expected auras to multiply as the genre's do, and made them one. The HUD lists the sources beside the stat ([ui.md](../ui/ui.md)).
- **Trading makes the economy a market:** control supply (drop rates, limited items, untradeable starters), add item sinks (fusion consumes copies), keep trades atomic and server-side with a log, and block trading paid items where `IsPaidItemTradingAllowed` is false.
- **Chance arithmetic** for a 1-in-N tier at luck L: chance per roll L/N; half of players have it after about 0.69 N/L rolls, 90% after about 2.3 N/L; after N/L rolls 37% still have none, after 3 N/L 5%. Set target times on the median and the 90th percentile, not the mean. Pity at 2-3x the expected rolls rescues only the unluckiest 5-14% and removes the worst stories. Cap stacked luck (luck × server luck × weather × passes multiplies fast) so the top tier stays rare. The odds shown come from the same function and data the server rolls with. A guaranteed item per banner multiplies across banners, so size pity at the pool you will ship: a pity of 50 suited a six-item pool, and at the launch pool finished the collection in 4.5 h against a 25 h target. A paid pull is worth buying only when free pulls are scarce against the chase: at a free faucet of 11-19 pulls a day every free player got every headliner within its window, so paid pulls changed nothing. Measure the faucet from real session and match lengths (real matches ran 135 s against a modeled 75 s, halving free pulls), then simulate free and paying players per banner window before setting pity and prices.
- **Simulate, don't guess.** Copy [scripts/PacingSim.luau](../../scripts/PacingSim.luau) to `tools/pacing/` beside a `model.luau` *(default)* and run it with Lune. Require the game's constants modules where they are pure data, so the plan and the game share one source of truth. Model a prestige as a `save` buy whose `buy` resets the run and adds the bonus, chance as `math.random` in `step`, real-time timers in `away`, and saving for a zone as a `save` buy that becomes available once it is a few minutes of income away. Run `simulate` with the cheapest and the value player (`simulateMany` when there is chance) and paste `report`, `curve` and `rngTable` into the plan. Where buys add progress and no income (packs in a collection game), `value` has nothing to rank: give each buy a `worth` (its expected new cards or power) and run the `worth` player instead. Re-run whenever the numbers change.
- **No Lune:** the blueprint's `rokit.toml` does not pin it, and adding it makes Rokit ask the owner to trust `lune-org/lune`, an approval only they give. Meanwhile `rojo build` a copy, open it in its own Studio window, run the model with `python scripts/studio_mcp.py luau model.luau --studio <that copy>` and a first line `--@use PacingSim`, and close only that window after. Write `local PacingSim = PacingSim or require("./PacingSim")` so the model runs both ways. Where Rokit already holds a Lune binary, an offline runner works without the manifest: [studio-testing.md](../code/studio-testing.md#specs-in-studio).
- **Track the economy:** `AnalyticsService:LogEconomyEvent(player, flowType, currencyType, amount, endingBalance, transactionType, itemSku, customFields)` for every source and sink, on the server after the transaction succeeds, never on the attempt. `amount` is always positive (the dashboard shows sinks as negative); `endingBalance` is the balance after it. Start from `Enum.AnalyticsEconomyTransactionType` (`IAP`, `TimedReward`, `Onboarding`, `Shop`, `Gameplay`, `ContextualPurchase`, sent as `.Name`) or name your own, and keep types and SKUs stable: past 20 transaction types and 100 SKUs the rest group as "Other". Plan for five currencies: the economy page shows five, the limits table allows 10 resource types. Sources minus sinks should stay near zero; a growing net or a rising average wallet, payers' above all, calls for sinks. A checkout funnel measures the steps to a purchase; the economy event is its ledger ([docs](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/analytics/economy-events)).

## Pacing models

A model that leaves a source out or reads the wrong number agrees with itself and is wrong. Check these before trusting its result ([Simulate](#economy-and-pacing)).

- **Model the free player's whole stack, AFK included:** one model counted the upgrade set, the level multiplier and the free pad at full-time training and left out AFK (a quarter of the time away, up to 6 h a day, at every multiplier). For a player at an hour a day, AFK was 3-6x their active EXP, and with pets (to x101) and auras (to x6) a free player reached the top in about 4 h of play against a designed 85 h; one day's AFK claim jumped 40 levels at once. Model pets with their tiers by copies, auras, drops and AFK at an assumed daily play time, and run a measure pass against the game's caps before tuning anything.
- **Read the payout code before modelling income per run or per claim:** an assumption that a run pays every stage it passes summed stages 1-80 (9,960 for a curve of +3 a stage), and the owner asked how stage 80 could pay that much. The pad paid its own map only (one line in the claim function). The project's own price helper, named for "a run's" income, summed every map too, so mid-game prices were far harder than designed: the last upgrade cost 54 claims against a designed 4 runs, and all thirty about 800 claims against 67. Measure the faucet from what one claim actually pays, never from a helper's name or comment.
- **Restate a user's formula with its index, and reproduce their number first:** "6 + 6 x (0.5 x stage - 1)" beside "50% more per stage" read two ways (+3 each stage or x1.5 each stage: 249 against 489 trillion at stage 80), both were tabled, and the wrong one led. The user then put stage 80 into the per-upgrade formula (954), which counts upgrades (30), not stages. Writing each formula as an explicit expression with its index variable, and matching the user's own worked example before modelling alternatives, would have saved two rounds.
- **Step a simulation at the curve's finest unit, one level, not the player's action cycle:** sampling the rate once per 2-minute claim cycle gave early caps steps of up to x2.8 between neighbouring levels (several 45 s levels share a cycle), and the measure pass ran 25% slow early because level-ups mid-cycle were not applied. Taking only the bought gear from the simulation and each level's own multiplier and pad, and stepping EXP level by level inside a cycle, gave a largest step of x1.8 and matched the design within minutes from the first level.
- **Settle a currency that buys its own multiplier by iteration:** pets bought with the soft currency add a bonus to it, and runes add more; on an 85 h designed clock the unchecked loop drove pets to their x101 cap by level 275 and the currency's multiplier to x160. Pricing on the median multiplier of each stage (the model divides the table's boost out of each price and repeats the design pass) settled within the 3-9% noise of random hatches in 3-10 passes, with upgrades back on schedule (set 23 at level 200 instead of 30).
- **Model both play styles before tuning stage difficulty:** where EXP is never capped and evolving spends one cap, a script printed per stage the seconds of training to the next stage's recommended EXP at the model's rate, and the lowest evolution whose cap covers it. Evolving players needed 3-4 evolutions a stage (minutes), but players who trained past max level reached the next stage in 11-16 s in the first world, and real players with pets, auras and passes earn 5-20x the model. A literal "2 minutes a stage" would have needed x2-5 per stage, thousands-fold over a world. A per-world exponential instead (0.6 to 1.8 of the paced cap, +6% a stage) left the first world about unchanged to stage 5, raised each world's end by 16-22 evolutions and eased each world's start by 6-11: the sawtooth asked for. Write a spec's step bound relative to the cap curve's own steps (x2-2.9 where a new training tier opens).
- **Check that the model reads the knob being tuned:** the pacing model farmed stages by paced evolution, so every change of recommended level measured as zero. A scratch copy whose player farmed the furthest passable gate showed the final curve taking the last gate from 144 to 119 hours, without touching the held table. Before trusting an unchanged result, confirm the simulated player follows what changed.
- **When a new world's numbers sit flat, check egg and upgrade prices before the item's power:** chart the median multiplier per checkpoint. The median pets multiplier stayed flat for about 75 modelled hours into a new world; raising the pet step from x1.5 to x2 alone left about 50 flat hours, while cutting the egg's price share at the same time made it climb through the whole world (x53 to x177).

## Ladders and curves

Check each change below with the pacing model, before and after.

- **Front-load growth with one shared lead:** a flat per-level curve hides the early levels (one shared 3.5% curve gave +1% total damage early, the player's weapon doing two thirds of it). Multiply the stat curve, the enemy HP, the income that follows it (gold per break) and the upgrade prices by the same lead at level = stage (+15% a level, then +7%, for the first 20): the first milestone moved from 45 s to 2.2 min while every later milestone and the day-84 end state stayed identical. Leave health and enemy attack on the steady curve, so hits to a knock-out do not change.
- **Re-cost a ladder by what the copies bought:** when its tiers cost more copies, give each tier the power the same copies bought on the old ladder; keeping each tier index's multiplier and level cap slowed stage 200 from day 32 to 58 and pushed stage 250 past day 84, where the old ladder's power per copy put every stage milestone back within a few days. Each step still pays less per copy as it climbs (13% a copy at the second star, under 1% at the top band). A system sharing the tier table (equipment) gets its own power columns, or re-mapping it cuts what an item arrives with (a new top-rarity weapon: 31% of its power, its level cap from 100 to 60).
- **Giving an item more stats keeps its total budget** *(default)*: single-stat items were given one to three extra stats worth 10 to 45 budget points and kept their original stat unchanged, while the owner expected that stat cut in balance. Price the new stats, cut the old one in the same change and report the numbers (a later pass cut it to 0.8 of its value, which lowered the equipped total 6% early and 19% late), or say plainly that it is a buff.
- **In a live game, replace a hard bonus cap with a soft limit that starts at the old cap and grows with each world:** hard caps made later worlds' pets, runes and gear worth nothing past a mid ascension. A limit equal to the live cap, with the surplus counted through a root, never lowers anyone. Choose the exponent from the highest real payer: the square root would have taken a maxed payer from x151 to about x410 at once, the cube root to about x294. A spec holds the old caps and checks that no total counts for less.
- **Start the best pull a few tiers in, then re-model the stages:** a best pull that arrives looking maxed leaves no climb, but each tier caps the unit's level, so moving its start from tier 5 to 3 of 14 slowed the 100th stage from 1 h to 4.7 h of play. Lift the low tiers' caps (by 20 there: back to 2 h, the climb still slower, the first star of the next grade from day 1 to 11 and the top tier from about day 112 to 155) or the stage curve.
- **Open a unit's commitment system where its material has stocked up:** at a mid-ladder tier of the unit's own, one band per tier after it, never the top. Gated at the first prestige tier, the owner's first idea, it opened on days 44 to 53 with about 620 of its material unspent (1,300 by day 180) and its last band past day 160; at mid-ladder, on days 19 to 23 with stock for the first band at once, and the prestige tier still completes it. Model the unspent material at the gate.
- **Start a soft currency at 1 and grow it by a shrinking ratio** *(default)*: per-kill income n^log2(3) of the stage n (each doubling of the stage triples it: 1, 3, 6, 10, 12 ... 1,000 at stage 80, near the old curve's end of 1,200), so early numbers start small and scale better. Cut every price from a full run's income where it is bought, so each keeps its place in the pacing (fixed prices of 1.5 runs to map 5 and 2 runs to maps 20 and 40 kept those proportions). Specs read amounts from config: two integration specs with the old amounts written in failed until they did. Those proportions were later too steep for the second item: the next item.
- **Price a ladder of multiplier collectibles at about one run's income where each is bought** *(default)*: at 1.5 runs to map 5 and 2 runs to maps 20 and 40 (590, 23,000 and 130,000), the owner found the second far too costly and asked for about 250 for the first and around 1,000 for the second. Re-priced at 0.64 of a run to map 5 (250), 0.9 of a run to map 8 (1,000), then about one run at each item's world and map, each step 1.4-1.9x the last.
- **Price an upgrade ladder on the time between buys, not on claims:** on an EXP schedule where each evolution takes x1.0158 longer (about 3.7 a stage, so each stage about 6% longer), upgrades priced as claims at their stage (pay x1.2, price x1.24) cost 66-157% of the currency earned on the way in the first world but only 10-30% in the fourth, so late upgrades would be bought hours early. Growing prices by the pay ratio times the time growth (x1.0625 a stage on top of the pay) held a median 0.6-0.74 of the currency earned on the way across all four worlds. Check price / currency earned between purchases.
- **Derive every soft-currency price as a share of one anchor curve at its own stage:** after the upgrade ladder changed shape, keeping each aura, egg and pack at its old number of claims would have made an early aura 5-6 upgrades' worth (it was half) and an owned-aura refund 6.6 upgrades (it was 0.54). Keeping each at its old share of an upgrade at its stage kept every relation and gave sensible claims (auras 0.4-1 claim mid-game, 26 for the last), and a curve change moves them all together.
- **A falling per-stage multiplier reaches its floor where early play ends:** `max(1.15, 2 - 0.02 x stage)` stays above x1.5 to stage 25 and compounds to 214 billion at stage 80 (a flat x1.2 ends at 86 million): early doubling builds the base before the decline arrives. Falling 0.05 a stage reached the floor at stage 17 and ended at 96 million, while the first world still nearly doubled each stage. Model the end value before choosing the fall rate.
- **After rounding a rising curve, enforce its minimum step again:** a per-stage reward curve took max(curve ratio, +6%) over the previous stage and then rounded to two significant figures. Below a power of ten the rounding unit is 1% of the value, just past it 10%, so 980 x 1.06 = 1,039 rounded to 1,000: a +2% step that a spec asking at least +3% caught (map 57). Looping "while value < last x 1.03, take the next two-figure value" fixed it (980 to 1,100); the smallest step became +4.2%. The older "nice numbers" rounding (1, 1.2, 1.5, 2, 2.5 ...) had tied neighbours outright (five stages at 300, eleven at 800).
- **Hook new players early, then ease to the normal pace** *(default)*: milestones that change the look at progression levels 5, 10 and 20, then every ten; the first upgrade sets about 70% cheaper, a price share of 0.3 for sets 2-4 easing to 1 by set 12 (set 2 from 1,400 to 410 of the currency). Even that was too slow for the owner, who wanted the second or third upgrade after one or two runs: price the first purchasable upgrade at about one early run's income (50 where a run reaching map 2 collects 48), the next at 140, then each at a few runs of what a player collects where they are expected to be, up to 1.6M for the last. Scale later prices up from the first, not down from the top of the curve.
- **Paid boosts stay out of the expected-player model:** moving the last three training multipliers (x5, x6, x8, at levels 70, 120 and 200) behind passes, and deriving the model's gear from free stations only, lowered the stat caps by 20% at level 70, 33% at 120 and 50% from 200, so free players keep the designed times between milestones. Then re-check what is derived from the model and rounded: a recommended stat rounded to two figures tied on neighbouring maps (62 and 63 both 1.3M) and a "rises map to map" spec failed; stepping a tie up one two-figure notch fixed it. Copies baked into the place go stale too: 60 of 80 gate signs had to be refreshed in place from the new config.
- **Never drop content to fit a new schedule; show the arithmetic and ask:** given new gaps between form milestones (5 levels four times, then 10 four times, 15 four times, then 20) under a cap of 300, only 22 of 30 forms fit, so eight were taken off the path (kept as shop sets), and the owner wanted every form back, only the goal for each moved. The 30th form needed 460 on those gaps. Asked with three options (shrink the late steps, scale every gap, raise the cap), the owner chose shrinking the late steps: 5 four times, 8 four times, 10 four times, then 12, the last form at 296. Keep every piece of content unless told to cut it.
- **Unlock squad slots a whole squad at a time** *(default)*: all four slots of the first squad from the start, each later squad whole. The units in early slots matter more than their count: with weak common units in the extra slots, only the first milestone moved (2.2 to 1.5 min).
- **Fix the count of elite or bonus enemies per wave when players compare run rewards:** the owner reported rewards changing inconsistently without changing pets. Each of 8 enemies rolled elite (3x) at 1 in 4, so a run paid anywhere from 8 to 24 normal enemies' worth. Exactly 2 elites per wave (the old average) keeps the mean and makes every run of a stage pay the same; which enemies are elite is still shuffled. A per-unit random multiplier makes a shown or compared total vary.
- **Judge a stage-difficulty curve by its longest single wait in hours, not its average level step:** late worlds' average steps (8.7 and 9.0) matched the liked world's (8.7), yet single stages took 10 and 15 hours: the levels copied the EXP cap's lumps (+29, +42) where a level costs most. Copying the liked world's steps plus one kept a +23 and a 9-hour wait. An even staircase (+8 to +13) cut the longest waits to 4.1 and 5.6 hours with almost the same world ends. For gates on an exponential level curve, table each gate's play time and longest wait before and after.
- **When gates move, re-check what the next area locks behind progression:** easier gates cut the gap between reaching a world and the evolution that unlocks its first training tool from 26 to 3; a variant that crossed it left the training multiplier at x1 and modelled an 82-hour wall. Keeping world ends near their old levels restored margins of 20 to 26.
- **Agree late-game added hours as a number first:** a request for additional content that takes more to push through was built as a rising curve of 170-200 added hours, each set a longer grind than the last, with a spec to enforce it; the owner found it a little too steep and asked for around 100. Flattening meant new caps, prices, 100 gate signs, the spec and every doc quoting them. Propose the total added hours and the curve's shape as numbers before building, and never read "a push" as an ever-steeper curve.

## Live ops and content

- Plan the cadence from day one: an update every 1-2 weeks with an in-game countdown, an event each month, seasonal events, team-hosted live events (server-wide weather, bosses or luck the team triggers).
- **Content is data:** a hero, zone, item or event is a data row plus assets, not new code, so an update is mostly content. Estimate the cost of one content unit (model, data, effects) and fit the roadmap to the team. Each new unit gets its own new assets *(default)*: reusing existing clips or effects to make new content cheaper is the user's call, so offer it as an option, never plan it in.
- **Roadmap along the direction:** 6-12 weeks, each update a headline, its content and the one twist it adds.
- Limited-time power never disappears for good: events come back, and event currency rules are clear.
- **A countdown ends when the item really leaves:** scan the rotation forward until it is no longer offered, since a rerun can bring a banner straight back and a naive end-of-week countdown would end while it stays.
- **A timed event's stay stays under its interval:** bosses stood 15 minutes on a 30-minute clock; at a 15-minute interval the stay would equal the gap, so a boss would always stand, the countdown would vanish, and each boundary would find the last boss still there and skip. The stay went to 10 minutes, leaving a 5-minute countdown. When changing a schedule's interval, check its duration against it and say so.
